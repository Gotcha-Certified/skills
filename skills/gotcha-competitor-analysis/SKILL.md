# Gotcha Competitor Analysis

Find where a competitor appears to outperform the user's company, where the user's company is already stronger, and what the team should investigate or test next.

Do not produce a generic website review. Follow real customer behaviour and gather evidence.

## 1. Start with three easy questions

Ask these in one short numbered batch:

1. Which company should I investigate? Share its name and website.
2. Which competitor should I compare it with? Say `find one` if you want me to choose.
3. What would you most like to understand or improve? For example: being found, convincing customers, buying or signing up, pricing, reviews, advertising, or something else.

Use the answers and available project context before asking anything else.

If no competitor is supplied, find plausible candidates and choose the one that creates the most useful comparison. Explain the choice in one sentence.

Ask follow-up questions only when an answer materially changes the research. Make each question concrete and easy to answer. Do not ask for a business strategy essay.

## 2. Confirm the scope

Summarise the assignment in one compact statement:

> I will compare `[company]` with `[competitor]` from the perspective of `[customer or business goal]`, focusing on `[part of the experience]`. I will show what the competitor appears to do better, what your company does better, and what your team should test next. Shall I start?

Describe the outcome, not the tools or every step.

## 3. Research the experience

Use public evidence by default. If the user has authorised private data or sources, use those where they improve the answer.

Choose the research route that fits the company and question. Relevant evidence may include:

- Search results and how customers first discover each company.
- Websites, product pages, pricing, offers, onboarding, booking, checkout, or contact flows.
- Public reviews and recurring customer language.
- Public advertising, including the Meta Ad Library when relevant.
- Help centres, product documentation, policies, delivery, returns, or support promises.
- Public company information and market signals.

Actively visit and inspect the relevant experiences. Use web browsing, visual inspection, Computer Use, and parallel research agents when they improve speed or coverage.

For a customer journey, follow the same realistic goal at both companies. Keep the scenario, budget, location, device, and stopping point comparable. Do not make a purchase, send a form, create a binding account, or cross another irreversible step without permission.

Capture evidence as you work:

- Save screenshots for important moments.
- Keep the page URL and research date.
- Record blocked, inaccessible, or untested steps.
- Keep public facts separate from interpretation and assumptions.

Do not decide the winner in advance. Preserve strengths in the user's company and weaknesses in the competitor.

## 4. Find actionable differences

Look for moments that can change a real business outcome:

- Can the customer find the right page?
- Does the landing experience match their intent?
- Is the next step clear?
- Is the offer easy to understand and trust?
- Is there unnecessary choice, friction, or delay?
- Does the competitor remove uncertainty earlier?
- Is one company better at showing proof, price, availability, delivery, or support?

For every important finding, provide:

1. **Observation** - what was directly visible.
2. **Why it may matter** - the likely effect, clearly labelled as interpretation.
3. **Evidence** - screenshot, source, date, and link.
4. **What to verify** - the internal data, customer research, or team knowledge needed.
5. **Next move** - a small test, question, or improvement the team can act on.

Prefer a few strong findings over a long list of surface-level differences.

If access to analytics, customer interviews, sales data, or funnel data would make a conclusion stronger, say exactly what to inspect. Do not stop merely because that private evidence is unavailable.

## 5. Create the report

Create both:

```text
Competitor Analysis/
  YYYY-MM-DD-company-vs-competitor/
    Report.html
    Report.pdf
    Evidence/
```

Use `assets/report-template.html` as a visual starting point. Adapt it to the evidence and the user's company. The HTML and PDF must contain the same conclusions.

Put the executive conclusion first. Then include:

- The question and scope.
- Three to five strongest findings.
- What the user's company already does well.
- A short comparison of the observed journey or experience.
- Recommended actions, owner or team, and a first test.
- Internal data needed to confirm the hypotheses.
- Limits, blocked steps, and assumptions.
- Sources, links, screenshots, and research date.

Write for a leader who wants to take the report to a team meeting. Use plain language. Make it obvious what is proven, what is inferred, and what needs checking.

Render the PDF from the final HTML. Open both versions and verify that all images resolve, text is readable, links are present, and no content is clipped.

## 6. Hand over

Give the user:

- The three most important findings.
- The first action worth discussing with the team.
- Links to `Report.html` and `Report.pdf`.
- Any missing internal evidence that could materially change the conclusion.

Do not claim that public observation proves why revenue, conversion, market share, or customer behaviour changed. It creates hypotheses and a sharper place to investigate.
